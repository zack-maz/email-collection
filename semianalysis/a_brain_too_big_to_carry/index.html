Section 01

The Embodiment Problem

With LLMs, the hardware bends to the model — train the biggest thing you can, then figure out how to serve it. Robotics inverts this. Two constraints fix the hardware first, and the model has to fit what can be manufactured and mounted.

The first constraint is time. A robot runs real-time control loops measured in milliseconds. An LLM can be slow without changing its output; if a robot is slow, the world moves on and the action is obsolete by the time it executes.

The second is cost. When you use an LLM, it lives in a datacenter you don't pay for upfront. In robotics, the manufacturer builds both the compute and the body — every unit, upfront — which at scale means billions or trillions in capital.

The key inversion: LLM labs choose model size to optimize quality against a training budget. Robot labs choose model size by what their data can support and what fits on a Jetson within a latency budget. Robot model sizes tell you where the constraints sit, not where intelligence will settle.

Section 02

Layers of a Robot Model

A generalist robot runs three stacked layers, each with different latency requirements. The layer determines whether compute can leave the robot at all — not the model architecture, not the task difficulty.

Planning layer (top): interprets the instruction and scene, decides what to do next, issues subtasks or grasp poses. Runs at ~5 Hz — each cycle has 200 ms. This is where reasoning lives, and reasoning models are big.

Action / motion layer (middle): turns planner outputs into joint velocities, body poses, or action sequences. Runs at 20–100 Hz, so each cycle takes 10–50 ms.

Servo & safety layer (bottom): balance, contact response, actuator commands. Runs at 100–1000 Hz — each cycle is 1–10 ms. This layer can never leave the robot.

Section 03

The Silicon Reality

The supply chain is optimized for the datacenter, not the robot. NVIDIA's Jetson line — the best robot compute available today — is a thin sliver of its total output. The reason is economic: Blackwell datacenter GPUs earn mid-to-high 70% gross margins; Jetson modules earn mid-60s. With scarce leading-edge wafers, NVIDIA has every incentive to prioritize the datacenter.

Worse, robot silicon is converging onto the same process nodes as the datacenter. Jetson Orin ran on Samsung SF8, insulated from datacenter competition. Jetson Thor moved to TSMC N4 — the same node as Blackwell. Future generations track N3 and N2 alongside datacenter GPUs. A low-volume, lower-margin Jetson will always be competing from the back of the queue.

Section 04

Total Cost of Ownership

Silicon efficiency tells you which approach scales. TCO tells you which is cheaper to run. SemiAnalysis benchmarked three scenarios for a 96-robot fleet running RoboTTT-shaped inference:

One B300 GPU can serve 12 robots within a 500 ms deadline. The RTX 6000 Pro handles 4 per GPU. A Jetson Thor serves one robot per chip, by definition.

Utilization is the swing factor. All three scenarios are almost entirely fixed capital — the bill is the same whether the asset runs or sits idle. Cost per productive hour scales inversely with utilization. Industrial robots run ~40% of the time; B300 server pools run ~90%.

Section 05

Who's Doing What — and Why

On paper, off-robot compute wins on TCO. In practice nobody is at mass production yet, so TCO isn't what decides today. Companies have split into two camps based on how much generality the robot needs, how bad the wireless is, and how much of the customer's site they're allowed to change.

Section 06

Climbing the Network Wall

Nearly every company that keeps inference on-device gives the same reason: the network. Off-device inference requires solving three layered problems — embodiment, the network infrastructure itself, and the physical environment. The companies that have gone off-device got there by staffing multiple dedicated teams.

The four hard problems — and how to address them
ProblemWhy it's hardThe fix
Uplink scheduling WiFi was designed for downlink-heavy stationary clients. Robots invert this: video goes up, while the robot moves. Robot-aware access points that give each robot a reserved slot instead of contending for the channel.
Access point handoffs A typical router goes silent for 100 ms–several seconds during a handoff — mid-manipulation, this can floor a robot. Fast roaming with pre-association, synchronized to the robot's position and predicted path.
Jitter buffering Variable delay forces the robot to buffer to worst-case, making every action pay the max-latency penalty. Centralized timing authority, sub-millisecond synchronization, batched inference on a shared clock.
Uplink bandwidth Image sensors are nearly all of a robot's uplink. Camera feeds at inference frequency saturate consumer WiFi. 6 GHz spectrum (uncongested), perception compression, uplink MIMO chipsets, co-designed mainboard + AP.
The prerequisite for off-device compute: a small-to-medium sized, structured space where the operator controls RF conditions. Autonomous driving fails this; factories and homes pass it — but neither is easy.
